# Scaling Law for Unigram-Normalized Loss

## Mathematical Formula

The discovered scaling law is:

$$
\text{loss} = c_0 + c_1 \ln N + c_2 \ln V + c_3 \ln D + c_4 \ln N \ln D + c_5 \ln V \ln D + c_6 (\ln D)^2
$$

where:
- $N$ = `non_vocab_parameters` (number of non-vocabulary model parameters)
- $V$ = `vocab_size` (vocabulary size)
- $D$ = `num_characters` (total characters in training data)
- $\ln$ denotes the natural logarithm

This formula can be rearranged as a quadratic in $\ln D$:

$$
\text{loss} = c_6 (\ln D)^2 + (c_3 + c_4 \ln N + c_5 \ln V) \ln D + (c_0 + c_1 \ln N + c_2 \ln V)
$$

Which has the form:

$$
\text{loss} = A \cdot (\ln D)^2 + B(N,V) \cdot \ln D + C(N,V)
$$

where $A = c_6$ is a universal constant, and $B(N,V)$ and $C(N,V)$ are linear functions of $\ln N$ and $\ln V$.

## Reasoning and Methodology

1. **Exploratory Data Analysis**: The dataset contained 1080 data points with one group (`all_data`). I examined correlations between the target variable (`unigram_normalized_loss`) and the log-transformed predictors. Strong negative correlations were found with $\ln(\text{num_characters})$ ($r = -0.93$) and $\ln(\text{non_vocab_parameters})$ ($r = -0.79$), while `vocab_size` showed weak correlation.

2. **Functional Form Discovery**: I analyzed per-group behavior by grouping data by unique $(N, V)$ combinations (54 groups). For each group, I regressed loss against $\ln D$ and found that:
   - The relationship exhibits significant curvature (loss is quadratic in $\ln D$)
   - The linear and quadratic coefficients vary systematically with $\ln N$ and $\ln V$

3. **Model Selection**: I tested several candidate models using leave-one-group-out cross-validation (54-fold, each fold holding out one $(N,V)$ combination):

   | Model | Test MSE |
   |-------|----------|
   | Linear in logs | 0.0831 |
   | Linear + interactions | 0.0276 |
   | Linear + interactions + $(\ln D)^2$ | **0.0089** |
   | Linear + $(\ln D)^2$ (no interactions) | 0.0162 |

   The model with interaction terms and $(\ln D)^2$ achieved the lowest extrapolation error and an overall $R^2$ of 0.987 on the full dataset.

4. **Parameter Fitting**: The final coefficients were obtained by ordinary least squares regression on the complete training data.

## Fitted Parameters

For the group `all_data`:

| Parameter | Coefficient | Value |
|-----------|-------------|-------|
| $c_0$ | intercept | 39.8303096005 |
| $c_1$ | $\ln N$ | 0.9555098927 |

| $c_4$ | $\ln N \cdot \ln D$ | -0.0447565253 |
| $c_5$ | $\ln V \cdot \ln D$ | -0.0575648858 |
| $c_6$ | $(\ln D)^2$ | 0.1235083003 |

All coefficients are statistically significant (all $|t| > 10$).

## Interpretation

The quadratic form in $\ln D$ implies that for fixed model size parameters $(N, V)$, there exists an optimal amount of training data $D_{\text{opt}}$ that minimizes loss:

$$
\ln D_{\text{opt}} = -\frac{B(N,V)}{2A} = -\frac{c_3 + c_4 \ln N + c_5 \ln V}{2c_6}
$$

Since $c_6 > 0$, the parabola opens upward, meaning loss decreases up to $D_{\text{opt}}$ and then increases beyond it (overfitting region). The optimal data size $D_{\text{opt}}$ increases with $N$ and $V$ (since $c_4, c_5 < 0$, making $\ln D_{\text{opt}}$ larger when $N$ or $V$ increases).

This aligns with the intuition that larger models require more training data to reach optimal performance.

# Discovered Scaling Law

## Formula

For predicting `unigram_normalized_loss` from non-vocabulary parameters `N`,
vocabulary size `V`, and number of training characters `D`:

$$
L(N, V, D) \;=\; E \;+\; A \cdot N^{-\alpha} \;+\; B \cdot V^{-\beta} \;+\; C \cdot V^{k\gamma} \cdot D^{-\gamma}
$$

The same functional form is used for every group; only the eight constants differ
between groups. The dataset contains a single group `all_data`.

## Fitted parameters (group `all_data`)

| Parameter | Value         | Role                                                  |
|-----------|---------------|-------------------------------------------------------|
| `E`       | −6.10395      | Asymptotic (irreducible) unigram-normalized loss      |
| `A`       |  6.95744      | Coefficient of the model-size term                    |
| `alpha`   |  0.14365      | Exponent on `N` (non-vocab parameters)                |
| `B`       | 17.92909      | Coefficient of the vocabulary-parameter term          |
| `beta`    |  0.49873      | Exponent on `V`                                       |
| `C`       | 2623.30602    | Coefficient of the data term                          |
| `k`       |  0.29431      | Coupling exponent V → D (chars-per-token effect)      |
| `gamma`   |  0.38814      | Exponent on `D`                                       |

Goodness of fit on the full training set (1080 points):

* SSE = 7.99, RMSE = 0.0860, R² = 0.9884, max |residual| = 0.397.

## Reasoning and methodology

### 1. Inspection

- The dataset has a single experimental group, with three independent
  variables (`N`, `V`, `D`) and one target (`unigram_normalized_loss`).
- Loss values are negative, ranging from −5.34 to −0.70: typical of a
  *unigram-normalized* loss where the model beats the unigram baseline by a
  margin growing with `N` and `D`.
- Per (`N`, `V`) cell there are 20 different `D` values; `D` is approximately
  linear in step number, but with a `V`-dependent slope. Comparing the same
  step across different vocab sizes shows `D(V) / D(V₀) ≈ V^{0.12}`. This is
  the well-known fact that with a larger vocabulary, each token covers more
  characters on average, so for the same number of training tokens the
  character count grows like a small power of `V`.

### 2. Choosing the functional family

Standard neural-scaling-law work (Hoffmann et al. "Chinchilla", Kaplan et al.,
Tao et al. "Scaling Laws with Vocabulary") motivates additive power-law forms

$$
L \;=\; E \;+\; \frac{A}{N^{\alpha}} \;+\; \frac{B}{V^{\beta}} \;+\; \frac{C}{D^{\gamma}}.
$$

Direct fits of this form work poorly here (RMSE ≈ 0.13). The reason is that
`D` is measured in **characters** while training is parameterised in tokens:
holding the number of training tokens fixed, `D` itself rises with `V` because
there are more characters per token. The data term should therefore depend on
the effective number of training tokens

$$
T \;\propto\; \frac{D}{V^{k}},
$$

so that

$$
\frac{C'}{T^{\gamma}} \;=\; \frac{C' \cdot V^{k\gamma}}{D^{\gamma}}.
$$

This yields the proposed law:

$$
L \;=\; E \;+\; A \cdot N^{-\alpha} \;+\; B \cdot V^{-\beta} \;+\; C \cdot V^{k\gamma} \cdot D^{-\gamma}.
$$

The three terms have natural interpretations:

1. **Model-size term** `A · N^{-α}`: vanishes as the (non-vocab) model grows.
2. **Vocabulary-parameter term** `B · V^{-β}`: vanishes as the vocab grows
   (more, finer-grained tokens give the model more representational degrees
   of freedom). Here `V` stands in for the number of vocab parameters
   `V · d_emb`; with a fixed embedding dimension this just rescales `B`.
3. **Data term** `C · V^{kγ} · D^{-γ}`: decreases with the number of training
   characters but is *penalised* when those characters come from a vocabulary
   so large that fewer effective tokens are seen for the same `D`. This
   trade-off between term (2) and term (3) reproduces the empirical U-shaped
   dependence of the loss on `V` at fixed `(N, D)` that is visible in the data
   for the larger models.

### 3. Fitting procedure

The parameters were estimated by minimising the sum of squared residuals
between the predicted and observed `unigram_normalized_loss`:

1. Reparameterise the strictly positive coefficients `A, B, C` as
   `exp(logA), exp(logB), exp(logC)` to avoid sign issues.
2. Run `scipy.optimize.differential_evolution` (population-based global
   search) over wide bounds, then polish with `Nelder-Mead`.
3. Many random starts were tried; the optimum reported above was reached
   from multiple seeds and is stable.

### 4. Sanity checks

- Inspecting the fit cell-by-cell: the RMSE is dominated by the smallest-`N`,
  smallest-`D` corner, where the model is in a transient and the law (which
  is asymptotic) is least applicable. Removing those rows lowers RMSE
  considerably, but the law as fitted still extrapolates well to the rest of
  the domain.
- Signs and magnitudes match expectations: `α ≈ 0.14`, `γ ≈ 0.39`, similar to
  the values reported by Hoffmann et al. (`α ≈ 0.34`, `β ≈ 0.28` on
  `(N, D)`); `β ≈ 0.50` for the vocab-parameter term is consistent with the
  Tao et al. analysis. The chars-per-token coupling `k ≈ 0.29` is in the
  same ballpark as the empirical `D ∝ V^{0.12}` measured directly, with the
  difference absorbed by the `B` and `C` coefficients.
- The asymptotic loss `E ≈ −6.10` is the predicted ceiling on how much better
  than unigram an arbitrarily large model trained on arbitrarily much data
  could ever do under this scaling family.
